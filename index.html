A note on units

Rates in this guide are usually quoted as decimals ($r = 0.045$ means 4.5%), but when Australian market convention quotes them differently (e.g. 100 − yield for bond futures, yield in percent), the convention is flagged in-text. The single most common bug in fixed income work is mixing decimal and percentage; if a number looks off by 100×, this is your first suspect. The second most common bug is mixing daycount conventions (act/365 vs act/360 vs 30/360); we'll spell out the convention every time it changes.

A note on convexity

The word "convexity" appears with at least four distinct meanings in fixed income: (i) the second-derivative term in the bond price–yield expansion, (ii) the convexity adjustment between futures and forwards, (iii) the value-of-optionality in callable bonds and mortgages, and (iv) the curvature of any swap or FRA payoff. They are related — all are about non-linear payoffs — but they are not the same number. Whenever the word appears, the session will say which sense is meant.

The big picture

Almost everything in a rates market reduces to one question: what is the price today of a stream of cashflows in the future? Every product — a government bond, a swap, a futures contract, a repo, a CDS — is a particular packaging of future cashflows. Pricing it means discounting those cashflows back to today using the right discount factors.

Module 1.1The Plumbing of the Australian Rates Market

Before any pricing formula, before any trade, there is the question of who owns what, where the records live, and how money and securities actually move. The plumbing is invisible when it works, and the only thing that matters when it doesn't.

A government bond is a promise. The Commonwealth of Australia, via the Australian Office of Financial Management (AOFM), promises to pay the holder a fixed coupon every six months and the face value at maturity. But that promise is meaningless unless three things exist: a registry that records who currently holds the promise, a settlement system that can transfer the promise (and the cash to pay for it) between parties, and a set of conventions that make a number like "4.25% 21 April 2026" mean the same thing to every market participant.

This module is about those three things. We won't price a single bond. But every module afterwards assumes you know who AOFM is, what RITS is doing in the background of every trade, and why a "matured" bond and a "settled" bond are not the same. If the rest of the guide is the algebra, this module is the grammar.

1.1 Who issues

The Australian fixed income market has roughly \$2 trillion of outstanding bonds, divided between four issuer types. Knowing which type a bond belongs to immediately tells you most of what you need to know about its credit quality, liquidity, and how it trades.

The four issuer types
  1. Commonwealth Government Securities (CGS / "ACGB") — issued by AOFM on behalf of the Australian Treasury. AAA-rated by S&P (one of about a dozen sovereigns globally to still hold this), highly liquid, the benchmark against which every other AUD bond is priced. About \$900 billion outstanding.
  2. Semi-government bonds ("semis") — issued by state treasury corporations: NSW (TCorp), Victoria (TCV), Queensland (QTC), Western Australia (WATC), South Australia (SAFA), Tasmania (Tascorp). Implicitly backed by state governments, mostly AA-rated, trade at a small spread over CGS (the "semi spread," typically 20–40bp at the 10-year point).
  3. Supranational, Sovereign and Agency (SSA) — entities like the World Bank, EIB, KfW issuing AUD-denominated debt (Kangaroo bonds). High-quality but smaller and less liquid.
  4. Corporate & financial bonds — major Australian banks (CBA, NAB, Westpac, ANZ), insurers, infrastructure issuers, and offshore corporates issuing in AUD. Wide credit-quality range; less liquid than the public sector segment.
Why "ACGB" appears everywhere

The acronym stands for Australian Commonwealth Government Bond. On a Bloomberg screen, the line might read ACGB 4.25 04/26 — that's the Australian government's 4.25%-coupon bond maturing in April 2026. The number you trade is the yield; the price is computed from the yield using the AOFM pricing formula (Module 1.3). When a desk says "the 10-year ACGB" they mean the on-the-run (most recently issued) bond closest to 10-year maturity — currently the November 2034.

1.2 Who holds

On the other side of every government bond is a holder. The composition of holders matters because each cohort has different time horizons, different sensitivities to rate moves, and different reasons to buy. When a rumour says "the offshore bid is fading," that's a statement about which cohort is stepping back.

Why offshore share is unusual

Australia is an outlier among developed economies in how much of its government debt is held offshore — close to half. The reason is structural: Australia runs a persistent current account deficit (more imports than exports of capital), so foreigners accumulate AUD-denominated assets in aggregate. ACGB is the natural place to park them. The consequence is that AUD rates are unusually sensitive to global flows — when US Treasuries sell off, ACGB tends to follow, often beyond what local fundamentals would suggest. This matters in the cross-currency basis chapter.

1.3 Infrastructure: RITS, Austraclear, DvP

Suppose you buy \$10 million face value of the April 2026 ACGB from another dealer. The trade is agreed on the phone or on a screen at 10:14 am Sydney time. What happens between that moment and the moment the bonds actually appear in your account two days later?

Two parallel systems are involved. The cash leg runs through RITS, the Reserve Bank Information and Transfer System — this is the central bank's real-time gross-settlement (RTGS) system, where AUD payments between banks ultimately clear. The security leg runs through Austraclear, the central securities depository operated by ASX, where ownership of every Australian fixed income security is recorded.

Delivery versus Payment (DvP)

The two systems are linked: the cash moves only if the bond moves, and the bond moves only if the cash moves. Both legs happen simultaneously in a single atomic operation. This is called Delivery versus Payment, or DvP. The point is to eliminate principal risk — the risk that you send the bond and the counterparty fails to send the cash (or vice versa).

Figure 1.1 — DvP eliminates principal risk by linking the cash and security legs.

Settlement timing: T+2

The Australian convention is T+2: a trade agreed on Tuesday settles on Thursday (with weekends and public holidays skipped). The two-day gap exists to give the back office time to confirm trade details, allocate to client accounts, and arrange any needed funding. During those two days, the trade is economically done — both sides are on the hook — but it has not yet hit the registry.

"Failing" a trade

Occasionally a trade fails to settle on T+2 — most commonly because the seller doesn't have the bonds (they're holed up at another counterparty, or the seller themselves had a fail upstream). The seller is then short on settlement, and there's a recovery process involving repo borrowing of the missing bond (Module 3.3) and possibly a "fail charge." Persistent fails in a particular bond drive its repo specials behaviour — that bond starts trading at a special rate in repo because everyone wants to borrow it.

1.4 The RBA and the cash rate

Sitting at the centre of the system is the Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA). Its monetary policy job is to set the cash rate — the interest rate at which banks lend AUD to each other overnight, on an unsecured basis. Every other interest rate in Australia, directly or indirectly, is anchored to this one number.

The cash rate

The cash rate is a target set by the RBA Board, currently announced on the first Tuesday of most months. The realised cash rate — what banks actually transact at overnight — is measured and published the next day as AONIA (Australian Overnight Index Average). The target and the realised rate are usually within a basis point; the RBA's job (operationally) is to make sure they stay close.

How the RBA holds the cash rate at target

The RBA uses a corridor system. Banks have accounts (Exchange Settlement Accounts, or ESAs) at the RBA. The RBA stands ready to lend ESA balances overnight at target + 25bp and to pay interest on excess ESA balances at target − 25bp. These define an upper and lower bound on the interbank cash rate: no bank would lend at less than what the RBA pays on deposits (the floor), and no bank would borrow at more than what the RBA charges (the ceiling). The market clears somewhere inside the corridor, and the RBA fine-tunes the supply of ESA balances through open market operations to keep the midpoint at target.

Deep dive — why the corridor system is recent

Australia used to operate under a system where the RBA paid no interest on excess ESA balances, which meant banks had a strong incentive to lend any excess to each other (rather than leave it at zero) — and this competition pushed the cash rate toward the target. The system worked when ESA balances were small and the market was "thin." Post-2008 the RBA wanted to be able to inject very large amounts of liquidity (during the GFC, and again during 2020) without crushing the cash rate to zero. The fix was to pay interest on excess reserves (target − 25bp now, narrower at times) so that the cash rate would be anchored regardless of how much liquidity was sloshing around. Most major central banks have moved to similar systems.

The practical consequence: when the RBA cuts the cash rate, it doesn't physically force rates down — it just shifts the corridor. The interbank market then naturally re-clears inside the new corridor.

The transmission to other rates

The cash rate is the shortest rate in the economy — overnight, unsecured, between high-quality banks. Every other interest rate is some combination of (i) expectations of where the cash rate will go over the term of the loan, plus (ii) a risk premium for the credit and liquidity differences between the borrower and an overnight interbank loan. This is the expectations hypothesis (Module 2.2). It's not exactly true — risk premia move around — but it's the starting point for understanding any other rate you encounter.

Mental model

Picture the cash rate as the seed of a tree. The trunk is the OIS curve (a clean extrapolation of expected cash rates over time, Module 3.1). The major branches are BBSW (bank-credit-flavoured) and the government curve (credit-free, term-premium-flavoured). The twigs at the tips are corporate spreads, the basis, and the various idiosyncratic spreads of individual bonds. Cut the seed and the whole tree wilts. This is why every monetary policy decision in Australia matters more than any other single event for the rates market.

Module 1.2Time Value of Money

Every fixed income calculation reduces to one question: what is a dollar tomorrow worth today? The answer depends on three things — how long until tomorrow, what rate the market uses to discount, and how that rate compounds. Get these three right and bond pricing is arithmetic. Get any one wrong and you'll be wrong by hundreds of basis points.

A bond is a stream of future cashflows. To price it, we need a rule for converting a future dollar into a today dollar. That rule is the discount factor, and the discount factor in turn depends on a rate and a compounding convention. This module works through each of these objects, one at a time, with the goal of making you fluent in switching between them — because different parts of the market quote rates in different conventions, and the most common mistake in early fixed income work is forgetting which convention you're in.

2.1 Why conventions matter

Suppose someone says: "the one-year rate is 5%." This sentence alone is ambiguous. Does 5% mean: (i) you invest \$100 today and get \$105 in a year, with no intermediate compounding? Or (ii) you invest \$100 and get $\$100 \times (1 + 0.05/2)^2 = \$105.0625$ in a year, compounding semi-annually? Or (iii) you invest \$100 and get $\$100 \times e^{0.05} = \$105.127$ in a year, compounding continuously? The "5%" can mean any of these, and the resulting future value differs by 13 basis points between (i) and (iii).

A rate without a compounding convention is meaningless. Every quoted rate in fixed income comes with an implicit (and sometimes explicit) convention. The conventions you'll see most in the Australian market are:

The 360/365 trap

Australian markets predominantly use act/365 — the year has 365 days for interest calculations. US dollar markets predominantly use act/360. When you compare a USD rate to an AUD rate without adjusting for daycount, you introduce a bias of roughly $\tfrac{365}{360} - 1 \approx 1.39\%$ multiplicatively: a 5% USD rate quoted act/360 corresponds to about 5.07% on act/365. Always normalise before comparing. This is why every CIP calculation in Module 4.1 carries explicit daycount tags.

2.2 Simple compounding

Definition · simple interest

Under simple compounding, a principal $P$ invested at rate $r$ for time $t$ (in years) grows to

$$ FV = P \cdot (1 + r \cdot t). $$

No interest is paid on accumulated interest within the period — interest only accrues on the original principal.

Simple compounding is the right convention when the period is short and there's no re-investment of intermediate cashflows. A 90-day bank bill pays the full interest at maturity, not in instalments — so simple compounding is the natural way to price it. The same goes for repo (Module 3.3), where you borrow cash for a fixed term and pay interest once at the end.

Example — a 90-day bank bill

A 90-day bank bill has face value \$100. The bill is discount-priced: you pay $P$ today and receive \$100 in 90 days. The implied yield $r$ at act/365 satisfies

$$ 100 = P \cdot \left(1 + r \cdot \tfrac{90}{365}\right), \quad \text{so} \quad P = \frac{100}{1 + r \cdot \tfrac{90}{365}}. $$

If $r = 4.5\%$, then $P = 100 / (1 + 0.045 \cdot 90/365) = 100 / 1.01110 = \$98.902$. You pay \$98.90 today, receive \$100 in 90 days, and the implied simple rate is 4.5%.

2.3 Periodic (discrete) compounding

Once a deposit lasts long enough that interest is paid at intervals shorter than the term, you need a way to handle re-investment of intermediate cashflows. The standard treatment is periodic compounding.

Definition · periodic compounding

A principal $P$ at nominal rate $r$ compounded $n$ times per year for $t$ years grows to

$$ FV = P \cdot \left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}. $$

Here $r$ is the annual nominal rate, and each compounding period earns $r/n$ of the running balance.

The most important case in Australian fixed income is $n = 2$ — semi-annual compounding — because that's how bond coupons are paid (twice a year) and how the AOFM bond-pricing formula treats the yield. When someone says "the 10-year ACGB yields 4.20%," they mean 4.20% semi-annually compounded; the per-period rate is 2.10%.

Example — semi-annual vs annual

Invest \$100 at a nominal rate of 6% for 5 years. Compare annual and semi-annual:

$$ FV_{\text{annual}} = 100 \cdot (1.06)^5 = 133.82. $$ $$ FV_{\text{semi}} = 100 \cdot (1.03)^{10} = 134.39. $$

The semi-annual answer is higher: same nominal rate, but more frequent compounding lets you earn interest on interest sooner. The effective annual rate for the semi-annual case is $(1.03)^2 - 1 = 6.09\%$, the so-called effective annual rate (EAR).

Converting between conventions

To convert a semi-annual rate $r_{sa}$ to an equivalent continuous rate $r_c$, equate growth factors over one year: $(1 + r_{sa}/2)^2 = e^{r_c}$, giving

$$ r_c = 2 \ln\!\left(1 + \tfrac{r_{sa}}{2}\right). $$

To convert simple-act/365 to semi-annual, equate $(1 + r_{simple} \cdot t) = (1 + r_{sa}/2)^{2t}$ and solve. For long maturities these conversions matter; for short ones they are tiny.

2.4 Continuous compounding

Take periodic compounding to its limit by letting $n \to \infty$ (compounding infinitely often). Using the standard identity $\lim_{n \to \infty}(1 + r/n)^n = e^r$:

Definition · continuous compounding $$ FV = P \cdot e^{rt}. $$

The discount factor is the inverse:

$$ Z(0, t) = e^{-rt}. $$

Continuous compounding is not used to quote rates in any major market — no one walks up to a screen and sees a continuous yield. But it is used internally in almost every quantitative model. The reason is mathematical: the exponential $e^{rt}$ composes cleanly under sums of times ($e^{r(t_1 + t_2)} = e^{rt_1} \cdot e^{rt_2}$), which makes the algebra dramatically simpler, especially when rates are themselves stochastic. The Black-Scholes formula, Hull-White short-rate models, the Heston volatility model — all are stated in continuous compounding.

Deep dive — why the exponential composes and the periodic form doesn't

Under continuous compounding, the growth factor for time $t$ is $e^{rt}$. To split this into two pieces of length $t_1$ and $t_2$ with $t_1 + t_2 = t$: $$e^{rt} = e^{r(t_1 + t_2)} = e^{rt_1} \cdot e^{rt_2}.$$ The single rate $r$ describes both sub-periods. The same is not true for periodic compounding: $(1 + r/n)^{n(t_1 + t_2)}$ does factor as $(1 + r/n)^{nt_1} \cdot (1 + r/n)^{nt_2}$ only because we used the same $r$ for both pieces. As soon as the rate depends on time (which it does — that's a yield curve), the periodic form generates awkward algebra: you need to track which rate applied to which sub-period, and the product expression for the discount factor over a long maturity becomes a nested cascade. The exponential form handles this elegantly because $-\int_0^T r(s)\,ds$ is just a single number, and its exponential is the discount factor.

2.5 The discount factor

The discount factor $Z(0,T)$ is the price today of one dollar to be paid for certain at time $T$. It is the master object of fixed income pricing: every bond is just a weighted sum of discount factors at the bond's coupon dates.

Definition · discount factor

$Z(0,T)$ = present value today of \$1 paid at $T$, assuming the cashflow is risk-free. Equivalently, it's the price of a zero-coupon bond of maturity $T$ with face value 1.

For continuous compounding at flat rate $r$: $Z(0,T) = e^{-rT}$.
For semi-annual compounding at flat rate $y$: $Z(0,T) = (1 + y/2)^{-2T}$.
For simple compounding (short tenors): $Z(0,T) = 1/(1 + r T)$.

The discount factor must be a number between 0 and 1 (assuming positive rates). It must be decreasing in $T$: a dollar further in the future is worth less today. The collection of discount factors $\{Z(0,T)\}$ across all maturities $T$ is called the discount curve; it's the central object built in Module 2.2 by bootstrapping.

Example — discount factor at 5 years

Suppose the 5-year zero rate is 4.20% on a continuously compounded basis. Then

$$ Z(0, 5) = e^{-0.0420 \times 5} = e^{-0.21} = 0.8106. $$

So a riskless \$100 promised 5 years from today is worth \$81.06 today, given that rate. Equivalently, the 5-year zero-coupon bond would trade at a price of 81.06 per 100 face.

Mental model

Think of $Z(0,T)$ as the "exchange rate" between time-$T$ dollars and today-dollars. Pricing a bond is just converting each future coupon back to today-dollars using the appropriate exchange rate and summing. The yield $y$ is the single exchange rate that happens to make the sum match the observed price — a compression of all the discount factors into one summary number.

2.6 Interpolating between curve points

In practice, you observe discount factors only at a finite set of nodes — the maturities where market instruments exist (3m, 6m, 1y, 2y, 3y, 5y, 7y, 10y, 15y, 30y for ACGB). For cashflows that don't land exactly on a node — and almost no cashflow does — you need to interpolate.

The naive approach is linear interpolation on the zero rate $r(T)$. This is what spreadsheets do by default. The problem with linear interpolation on rates is that it produces a discontinuous forward rate: between any two nodes the forward rate is constant, then jumps at the node. A discontinuous forward rate makes no economic sense and produces visible artefacts in any derivative whose price depends on forward rates (like FRAs).

Convention · exponential (log-linear on $Z$) interpolation

For nodes $T_1 < T < T_2$ with known discount factors $Z(0, T_1)$ and $Z(0, T_2)$:

$$ \ln Z(0, T) = \ln Z(0, T_1) + \frac{T - T_1}{T_2 - T_1} \cdot \left[\ln Z(0, T_2) - \ln Z(0, T_1)\right]. $$

Equivalently, interpolate linearly on the continuous zero rate $r(T) = -\ln Z(0,T)/T$, but weighting by $T$:

$$ r(T) \cdot T = r(T_1) \cdot T_1 + \frac{T - T_1}{T_2 - T_1} \cdot \left[r(T_2) \cdot T_2 - r(T_1) \cdot T_1\right]. $$

This is called exponential interpolation because the discount factor varies exponentially in $T$ between nodes — which is what continuous compounding at a piecewise constant forward rate produces. The forward rate between $T_1$ and $T_2$ is constant under this interpolation, but at least it's a sensible value (the geometric average of the rates at the endpoints).

Example — interpolating to 4-year

Suppose $Z(0, 3) = 0.875$ and $Z(0, 5) = 0.810$. Find $Z(0, 4)$ using exponential interpolation.

$$ \ln Z(0, 4) = \ln(0.875) + \tfrac{4-3}{5-3}\left[\ln(0.810) - \ln(0.875)\right] $$ $$ = -0.1335 + 0.5 \cdot (-0.2107 - (-0.1335)) = -0.1335 + 0.5 \cdot (-0.0772) = -0.1721. $$ $$ Z(0, 4) = e^{-0.1721} = 0.8419. $$

For comparison, linear interpolation on $Z$ directly would give $(0.875 + 0.810)/2 = 0.8425$ — very close, but not the same, and the difference shows up in the implied forward rate between years 3 and 4. The exponential method gives a smooth, economically meaningful forward.

Module 1.3Bond Pricing

A bond is a contract. The Commonwealth promises to pay you a coupon every six months and the face value at maturity. The price you pay today is, by definition, the present value of all those future cashflows discounted at some yield. The only question is which yield — and the only complication is the dance between settlement date, coupon date, and accrual.

Up to this point we've talked about single cashflows. A bond is a stream: a coupon $C$ every six months until maturity, plus the face $F$ at the end. Pricing a bond is just discounting each piece back to today and adding the pieces up. The mechanics are straightforward; the subtleties — clean vs dirty price, the AOFM formula, the difference between settlement date and value date — are where the bugs live.

3.1 The cashflow ladder

Suppose today is 15 July 2025 and you settle a position in the 21 April 2030 ACGB with a 4.25% coupon. The bond's cashflow ladder looks like this:

DateDays from settlementCashflow (per \$100 face)
21 October 202598\$2.125 (semi-annual coupon)
21 April 2026280\$2.125
21 October 2026463\$2.125
.........
21 October 20291559\$2.125
21 April 20301741\$102.125 (final coupon + face)

The coupon is half the annual rate per 100 face: \$4.25/2 = 2.125. There are 10 cashflows in total (9 coupons over the next ~5 years, plus the redemption with final coupon).

Bond price as sum of discounted cashflows

Let $CF_i$ be the cashflow on date $t_i$ (in years from settlement). Let $Z(0, t_i)$ be the discount factor for that date. Then the bond's price is:

$$ P = \sum_{i=1}^{n} CF_i \cdot Z(0, t_i). $$

This is the most general, market-consistent price. It uses the actual term structure of discount factors from the swap or government curve, not a single yield.

In practice, however, the bond market quotes bonds in yield, not price. So we need a single yield $y$ such that the bond's price, discounted at that yield, matches the market price. That single yield is the yield to maturity (YTM).

3.2 Dirty price, clean price, accrued interest

The price computed by summing all discounted cashflows is the dirty price — the actual cash amount the buyer pays. But the dirty price has an awkward feature: it jumps discontinuously at every coupon date. The day before a coupon, the bond has the upcoming coupon "baked in" to its value. The day after, that coupon has been paid out, and the bond's value drops by approximately the coupon amount. If you plotted dirty price over time on a stable yield curve, you'd see a sawtooth pattern.

This sawtooth makes yield curves look ugly and confuses casual price comparisons. The market's solution is to quote bonds in a clean price that smooths out the sawtooth, while still settling at the dirty price.

Definition · accrued interest and clean price

Let $\tau$ be the fraction of the current coupon period that has elapsed since the last coupon date. The accrued interest is:

$$ AI = C \cdot \tau, $$

where $C$ is the coupon amount for the current period (= annual coupon / 2 for semi-annual bonds). The relationship between clean and dirty is:

$$ \text{Dirty price} = \text{Clean price} + \text{Accrued interest}. $$

The dealer quotes the clean price; the buyer pays the dirty price; the difference is the coupon income the buyer is "buying" from the seller because the seller held the bond through part of the current accrual period.

Figure 3.1 — The dirty price (red) sawtooths at each coupon date; the clean price (green) is smoothed.
Example — accrued interest on a 4.25% ACGB

Bond: 4.25% coupon, semi-annual, coupons on 21 April and 21 October. Settlement: 15 July 2025. Last coupon: 21 April 2025. Next coupon: 21 October 2025.

Days from last coupon to settlement: 85 (April has 9 remaining + May 31 + June 30 + 15 days July). Days in current period: 183 (full April–October half-year). So $\tau = 85/183 = 0.4645$.

Accrued interest per \$100 face: $AI = 2.125 \times 0.4645 = \$0.987$.

If the dirty price is \$99.30, the clean price (the quoted price) is $\$99.30 - 0.987 = \$98.313$.

Why dealers quote clean prices

If they quoted dirty, every time you scanned a bond price you'd have to mentally subtract the accrued just to know whether the price had actually moved or whether it was just the coupon accruing. Clean prices isolate the market movement from the mechanical coupon accrual. But the cash that changes hands is always dirty — when the bond is delivered into your Austraclear account, you pay accrued.

3.3 Yield to maturity

Yield to maturity is the single discount rate that makes the bond's sum of discounted cashflows equal its observed (dirty) market price. Algebraically, for a bond paying coupon $C$ every six months and face $F$ at maturity $T$ semi-periods away:

Definition · yield to maturity (semi-annual compounding) $$ P_{\text{dirty}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{C}{(1 + y/2)^{i - \tau}} + \frac{F}{(1 + y/2)^{n - \tau}}, $$

where $n$ is the number of remaining coupon payments, $\tau$ is the fraction of the current period already elapsed, and $y$ is the YTM (annualised, semi-annual compounding).

Several things deserve attention here. First, YTM is implicitly defined — there's no closed-form expression; it's solved numerically (Newton's method, bisection). Second, $y$ is a single number that "summarises" the whole discount curve. If the actual term structure has 5-year rates at 4.0% and 10-year rates at 4.5%, the YTM of a 7-year bond trades somewhere in between, weighted toward whichever rate dominates that bond's duration. Third, YTM is the realised return of holding the bond to maturity only if you can re-invest every coupon at $y$ — an assumption that almost never holds in reality. This is the reinvestment risk embedded in any YTM quote.

Three relationships you must internalise
  1. Price and yield move opposite. If yield rises, the discount factors in the price formula get smaller, so the present value falls. This is the single most fundamental fact in fixed income.
  2. Coupon > yield ⇒ premium bond (price > 100). The bond is paying more than the market demands, so it trades above face.
  3. Coupon < yield ⇒ discount bond (price < 100). The bond is underpaying, so it trades below face. Coupon = yield gives par.

3.4 The AOFM pricing formula

The Australian government bond market uses a specific algebraic form for the price-yield relationship, published by AOFM. It's algebraically equivalent to the standard discrete formula above, but it's the form that appears on every Bloomberg screen and in every market participant's spreadsheet, so it's worth seeing it once explicitly.

The AOFM formula (per \$100 face) $$ P_{\text{dirty}} = v^{f}\left[g + g \cdot a_{\overline{n-1}|} + 100 v^{n-1}\right] $$

where

  • $g$ = coupon per period = annual coupon / 2 (in dollars per \$100 face)
  • $v = 1/(1 + y/2)$ = single-period discount factor at YTM $y$
  • $f$ = fraction of period from settlement to next coupon date = $1 - \tau$
  • $n$ = number of remaining coupon periods (including the one currently accruing)
  • $a_{\overline{n-1}|} = \dfrac{1 - v^{n-1}}{y/2}$ = annuity factor for the $n-1$ coupons after the next

Unpacking the formula: the bracket discounts everything to the next coupon date (where there are exactly $n$ remaining coupons), then $v^f$ pulls it back to settlement. The first $g$ inside the bracket is the upcoming coupon; the $g \cdot a$ piece is the PV-at-next-coupon of all subsequent coupons; the $100 v^{n-1}$ is the PV of the face value.

Deep dive — deriving the AOFM formula from first principles

Start with the cashflow ladder. After settlement there are $n$ coupon dates remaining, occurring at times (in years) $f \cdot 0.5, (f+1) \cdot 0.5, (f+2) \cdot 0.5, \dots, (f+n-1) \cdot 0.5$ from settlement, where $f$ is the fraction of a period until the next coupon. At YTM $y$ (semi-annual compounding), the discount factor for a time $t$ in years is $(1+y/2)^{-2t}$, or for a time of $k$ half-periods it is $v^k$ where $v = (1+y/2)^{-1}$.

The PV of all cashflows from settlement: $$ P = g v^f + g v^{f+1} + g v^{f+2} + \dots + g v^{f+n-1} + 100 v^{f+n-1}. $$

Factor out $v^f$: $$ P = v^f \left[ g + g v + g v^2 + \dots + g v^{n-1} + 100 v^{n-1} \right]. $$

The geometric sum $g + g v + \dots + g v^{n-1} = g(1 + a_{\overline{n-1}|}) = g + g a_{\overline{n-1}|}$ where $a_{\overline{n-1}|} = (1 - v^{n-1})/(y/2)$ is the standard annuity factor for $n-1$ periods. So we get the published AOFM form.

Worked example — pricing the 4.25% Apr-2030 bond

Settlement: 15 July 2025. Yield: 4.20% (semi-annual). Next coupon: 21 October 2025.

From the earlier accrual example: $\tau = 0.4645$, so $f = 1 - \tau = 0.5355$.
$g = 2.125$, $v = 1/(1 + 0.042/2) = 1/1.021 = 0.97943$, $n = 10$.
$a_{\overline{9}|} = (1 - 0.97943^9)/0.021 = (1 - 0.82828)/0.021 = 8.1772$.

$$ P_{\text{dirty}} = 0.97943^{0.5355} \cdot \left[2.125 + 2.125 \cdot 8.1772 + 100 \cdot 0.97943^{9}\right] $$ $$ = 0.98899 \cdot \left[2.125 + 17.376 + 82.828\right] = 0.98899 \cdot 102.329 = \$101.20. $$

Subtracting the previously calculated accrued of \$0.987 gives a clean price of \$100.21.

Module 2.1Duration, DV01, and Convexity

Once you can price a bond, the next question is: how much does that price move when yields change? The first-order answer is duration; the second-order correction is convexity. These two numbers are how every rates desk in the world measures risk — and they are how you decide how much of something to trade against something else.

A bond is a fixed stream of cashflows. When market yields move, the price changes — but by how much? You could re-price the bond from scratch at the new yield (and on a computer in 2026, you usually do). But before you do that, you want a shortcut: a single number per bond that tells you the magnitude of its price sensitivity to small yield moves. That number is duration, and a thousand variations on it are the daily language of any rates desk.

4.1 Macaulay duration

Imagine a bond's cashflows laid out on a timeline, with each cashflow's present value sitting at its date. Macaulay duration is the centre of gravity of that picture — the time-weighted average of when the cashflows occur, with weights given by their PV share of the total price.

Definition · Macaulay duration $$ D_{\text{Mac}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n t_i \cdot \text{PV}(CF_i)}{\sum_{i=1}^n \text{PV}(CF_i)} = \sum_{i=1}^n t_i \cdot \frac{\text{PV}(CF_i)}{P}, $$

where $t_i$ is the time (in years) to cashflow $i$, $CF_i$ is the cashflow amount, PV$(CF_i)$ is its present value at the bond's YTM, and $P$ is the bond's total dirty price.

The units of Macaulay duration are years. For a 5-year coupon bond at par, the Macaulay duration is typically around 4.5 years — less than the time to maturity, because some cashflows arrive earlier (as coupons). For a 5-year zero-coupon bond, Macaulay duration is exactly 5 years — the only cashflow occurs at maturity.

Figure 4.1 — Macaulay duration is the centre of gravity of the bond's PV-weighted cashflows. The face value at maturity is the biggest weight and pulls the centre toward the right.

4.2 Modified duration

Macaulay duration is a beautiful summary number but you don't directly use it for risk calculations. What you want is the percentage change in price for a given change in yield. That's modified duration.

Definition · modified duration

For a bond with YTM $y$ compounded $n$ times per year (semi-annual ⇒ $n=2$):

$$ D^* = \frac{D_{\text{Mac}}}{1 + y/n}. $$

The price sensitivity to yield is:

$$ \frac{1}{P}\frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = -D^*. $$

Equivalently, for a small yield change $\Delta y$ (in decimal):

$$ \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D^* \cdot \Delta y. $$
Why the \$1/(1+y/n)\$ correction

Differentiate the price formula $P = \sum CF_i (1+y/n)^{-n t_i}$ with respect to $y$: $$ \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \sum CF_i \cdot (-t_i)(1+y/n)^{-n t_i - 1} = -\frac{1}{1+y/n} \sum t_i \cdot \text{PV}(CF_i). $$ The sum is $P \cdot D_{\text{Mac}}$, so $\partial P / \partial y = -P \cdot D_{\text{Mac}}/(1+y/n)$. Dividing both sides by $P$ gives the modified duration formula.

The minus sign is the price-yield inverse relationship. The bigger the modified duration, the more sensitive the bond is to yield moves. A typical 10-year ACGB has modified duration around 8.5; a 30-year bond has duration around 17. Doubling the maturity doesn't double the duration because cashflows are still arriving in the same place — the duration approaches a limit as maturity goes to infinity (in fact, for a perpetual annuity at yield $y$, $D^* = 1/y$, so at $y=4\%$ a perpetuity has $D^* = 25$ years).

4.3 DV01 (PVBP)

Modified duration tells you the percentage price change. Sometimes — most of the time, actually, on a trading desk — you want the dollar change for a 1bp move. That's DV01 (Dollar Value of 01) or, equivalently in some markets, PVBP (Present Value of a Basis Point).

Definition · DV01 / PVBP $$ \text{DV01} = P \cdot D^* \cdot 0.0001. $$

Units: dollars per 1 basis point yield change, per face value used. If you've computed $P$ per \$100 face, your DV01 is per \$100 face. To get DV01 per million face, scale by 10,000.

Example — DV01 of a 10-year ACGB

Suppose the 10-year ACGB has dirty price \$98.50 (per \$100 face) and modified duration $D^* = 8.5$. Then per \$100 face:

$$ \text{DV01} = 98.50 \times 8.5 \times 0.0001 = \$0.0837. $$

Per \$1 million face, DV01 = \$837. So if you own \$100 million face of this bond, your portfolio DV01 is \$83,700 per basis point. A 5bp move down in yield makes you about \$418,500; a 10bp move up loses you about \$837,000. This is the number a risk manager cares about.

Mental model — DV01 as a "rate units" measure

DV01 converts an interest-rate move (in basis points) into a P&L (in dollars). It's the rates equivalent of "delta" in equity options. The whole risk-management language on a rates desk is in DV01 terms: "the book is long \$2 million DV01," meaning a 1bp rally makes them \$2m and a 1bp sell-off loses them \$2m. A 10y position is long DV01; a swap receiving fixed is long DV01; a futures short is short DV01.

4.4 Convexity

Modified duration is a linear approximation: it treats the price-yield curve as a straight line locally. But the actual price-yield relationship is curved — and the curvature matters when yields move by a lot.

Figure 4.2 — The price-yield curve is convex. The duration line under-estimates the price both above and below the reference point. The gap is convexity.
Definition · convexity $$ C = \frac{1}{P} \frac{\partial^2 P}{\partial y^2}. $$

Second-order Taylor expansion of price in yield:

$$ \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D^* \cdot \Delta y + \tfrac{1}{2} C \cdot (\Delta y)^2. $$

The convexity term is always positive for a standard fixed-coupon bond. The reason: the price function is decreasing and convex (curving upward) in yield, so the second derivative is positive. In economic terms, this means the bond's gains on rallies are larger than its losses on sell-offs of the same magnitude — a free asymmetric payoff. The market knows this; it builds the value of convexity into the bond's yield. All else equal, more convex bonds yield slightly less than less convex bonds.

Example — convexity matters at 50bp

Bond with $P = 100$, $D^* = 8.5$, convexity $C = 95$. For a 50bp parallel rate move ($\Delta y = 0.005$):

Duration-only estimate: $\Delta P/P \approx -8.5 \times 0.005 = -4.25\%$, so price falls to $\approx 95.75$.

With convexity correction: $\Delta P/P \approx -4.25\% + \tfrac{1}{2}(95)(0.005)^2 = -4.25\% + 0.119\% = -4.131\%$. Price falls to $\approx 95.87$ — about 12 cents better.

For a 100bp move, the correction is 4× as big (because $(\Delta y)^2$ scales): about 48 cents. By the time you're talking about 200bp moves, the duration approximation is wrong by several percentage points and convexity is no longer optional.

Deep dive — where convexity lives in the market

Convexity isn't just a Taylor-series remainder. It's a tradable feature of bonds, and portfolio managers actively manage convexity exposure. Long-dated bonds are more convex than short-dated bonds — at the same duration, a "barbell" position (some very long and some very short) is more convex than a single intermediate-maturity bond. Mortgage-backed securities have negative convexity (because the borrower can prepay when rates fall, capping the MBS holder's gains) — these are the hardest convexity hedging problems in the world.

Convexity also explains the convexity adjustment between futures and forwards (Module 3.2): a futures contract has a different convexity profile than a forward rate agreement because of daily margining, and the systematic difference shows up in the price.

4.5 Hedge sizing with DV01

The single most common application of duration on a trading desk is hedge sizing. Suppose you own a 10-year ACGB and want to hedge the interest-rate risk using the YM (3-year) or XM (10-year) bond futures contract. How many futures contracts do you sell?

Hedge-ratio formula

To hedge a position with DV01 $\text{DV01}_{\text{pos}}$ using a hedge instrument with DV01 $\text{DV01}_{\text{hedge}}$ per contract, you need

$$ N_{\text{contracts}} = \frac{\text{DV01}_{\text{pos}}}{\text{DV01}_{\text{hedge}}}, $$

rounded to the nearest integer (you can't trade a fractional futures contract). The sign of the position determines whether you buy or sell the hedge.

Example — hedging a \$50m position with XM futures

You're long \$50 million face value of the 10-year ACGB, which has DV01 of \$0.083 per \$100 face. Total DV01 of your position:

$$ \text{DV01}_{\text{pos}} = \tfrac{50,\!000,\!000}{100} \times 0.083 = \$41,\!500 \text{ per bp}. $$

The XM (10-year) futures contract has a notional of \$100,000 and roughly \$90 DV01 per contract (since it tracks roughly a 10y bond). To hedge:

$$ N = 41,\!500 / 90 \approx 461 \text{ contracts}. $$

Sell 461 XM contracts. A 1bp parallel rally lifts the bond by \$41,500 and the futures short loses \$41,490 — net P&L roughly zero, as intended.

DV01-neutral isn't risk-neutral

A DV01-matched hedge is neutral only to parallel moves of the yield curve. If the curve twists — say short rates rally and long rates sell off — your 10-year position will move differently from your 3-year-tracking YM futures hedge, and you'll have a P&L. Real hedges account for this with key-rate duration (DV01 broken out by bucket: 2y, 5y, 10y, 30y) and choose hedge instruments to match each bucket separately.

  • Convexity matters when $|\Delta y|$ is more than ~30bp; below that, duration is enough.
  • Hedge sizing: number of contracts = DV01(position) / DV01(hedge per contract).
  • DV01-matched hedges neutralise parallel shifts only — curve reshaping risk requires key-rate hedging.

Module 2.2The Yield Curve

Three curves describe the same information: the zero curve (rates by maturity), the par curve (yields on hypothetical coupon bonds), and the forward curve (rates between two future dates). They are algebraically equivalent and economically distinct. Mastering the translation between them is what separates a rates trader from a guesser.

Until now we've talked about pricing a single bond at a single yield. But the market doesn't give you a single yield — it gives you a continuum of rates across maturities, and that continuum carries information about what the market expects. The 2-year yield reflects expectations of the cash rate over the next 2 years; the 10-year yield does the same over the next 10. The gap between them is the "term premium" plus a slice of expected future rate moves. This module is about how to read that information and how to build the curve in the first place.

5.1 Zero, par, and forward — three views of the same thing

There are three equivalent ways to describe the term structure of interest rates. Each is more convenient for different purposes; each can be derived from the others.

The zero curve

A function $r(T)$ giving the yield on a hypothetical zero-coupon bond of maturity $T$. Equivalent to the discount-factor curve $Z(0,T) = e^{-r(T) \cdot T}$. This is the most fundamental object — every other curve is derived from it.

The par curve

A function $c(T)$ giving the coupon rate that would make a hypothetical coupon-bearing bond of maturity $T$ trade exactly at par (price = 100). Implicitly, $c(T)$ satisfies:

$$ 100 = \sum_{i=1}^{2T} \tfrac{c(T)}{2} \cdot Z(0, t_i) + 100 \cdot Z(0, T). $$

The par curve is what gets quoted for swap rates ("the 5-year swap is 4.20%") — a swap's fixed leg is exactly a coupon stream priced at par against the floating leg.

The forward curve

A function $f(T_1, T_2)$ giving the rate the market implies for a hypothetical loan starting at $T_1$ and ending at $T_2$. From the no-arbitrage condition (you should be indifferent between investing for $T_2$ directly or for $T_1$ then rolling to $T_2$):

$$ Z(0, T_1) \cdot e^{-f(T_1, T_2)(T_2 - T_1)} = Z(0, T_2) \quad \Rightarrow \quad f(T_1, T_2) = \frac{\ln Z(0, T_1) - \ln Z(0, T_2)}{T_2 - T_1}. $$

The instantaneous forward rate is the limit as $T_2 \to T_1$, equal to $-\partial \ln Z(0,T)/\partial T$.

The expectations hypothesis

A classical (and only approximately true) idea: the forward rate equals the market's expectation of the corresponding future spot rate. Under the strict version:

$$ f(T_1, T_2) = \mathbb{E}_0[\text{spot rate between } T_1 \text{ and } T_2]. $$

In reality, forwards differ from expected future spots by a term premium (compensation for holding longer-dated risk) and possibly by a convexity adjustment. The premium is small or zero in normal times and large when investors demand more compensation for duration (e.g. after 2022).

Example — extracting the 5y5y forward

Suppose the 5-year zero rate is 4.00% (continuously compounded) and the 10-year zero is 4.50%. The forward rate from 5y to 10y is:

$$ f(5, 10) = \frac{4.50 \times 10 - 4.00 \times 5}{10 - 5} = \frac{45 - 20}{5} = 5.00\%. $$

Reading: the market is implying that the average 5-year rate, 5 years from now, will be 5.00%. If the term premium is roughly 50bp for the 5y5y point, the implied "expected" rate is more like 4.50% — i.e., the market thinks 5-year rates will be roughly unchanged in 5 years, and the curve's upward slope mostly reflects compensation for risk rather than expected rate hikes.

5.2 Bootstrapping the curve

The market doesn't quote zero rates. It quotes:

  • Short-rate instruments: BBSW out to 6 months (Module 3.1)
  • Rate futures: bank-bill futures and SOFR futures (Module 3.2)
  • Swap rates: 1y, 2y, 3y, 5y, 7y, 10y, 15y, 20y, 30y

Bootstrapping is the procedure for converting these quoted instruments into the underlying zero rates (and discount factors). The idea is recursive: solve for the shortest zero rate first (where there's a direct instrument), then use that to solve for the next, then the next, building outward in maturity.

  1. The short end (≤ 3 months). The 1-month, 2-month, 3-month BBSW give you the zero rate directly — these are simple-interest instruments where the BBSW is the zero rate for that tenor (approximately, ignoring credit). From BBSW you immediately have $Z(0, 1m), Z(0, 2m), Z(0, 3m)$ via $Z = 1/(1 + r \cdot t)$.
  2. 3 months to 4 years: futures strip. Each bank-bill futures contract fixes the rate for a particular 90-day window in the future. Stringing together consecutive contracts gives you a chain of forward rates. Compound them sequentially with the short BBSW rate to get longer-dated discount factors. Apply the convexity adjustment (Module 3.2).
  3. 1 year onwards: swap rates. Each swap rate $c(T_n)$ for tenor $T_n$ is a coupon rate that prices a $T_n$-year par swap. Given all previous discount factors $Z(0, t_1), \dots, Z(0, t_{n-1})$, the par swap equation $$ 100 = \sum_{i=1}^{2T_n} \tfrac{c(T_n)}{2} Z(0, t_i) + 100 Z(0, T_n) $$ has exactly one unknown — $Z(0, T_n)$ — which you solve for. Then move to the next swap tenor and repeat.
  4. Interpolate between nodes. The bootstrap gives you discount factors only at the node maturities. For cashflows in between, use exponential interpolation (Module 1.2). The final output is a continuous discount curve.
Multiple curves, post-2008

Before 2008, you would bootstrap a single AUD curve from BBSW, futures, and swaps. After 2008, the credit difference between BBSW (bank credit) and OIS (overnight, near-risk-free) became large and persistent, and the industry split into a two-curve framework: an OIS curve for discounting (because OIS approximates the risk-free rate available for cash collateral) and a BBSW projection curve for forecasting forward BBSW rates needed to value floating legs. Every modern pricing system uses this split. The BBSW-OIS spread is itself a tradable variable (Module 3.1).

Toy bootstrap — three nodes

Given: 1-year BBSW = 4.40%, 2-year par swap = 4.20%, 3-year par swap = 4.10%. Assuming semi-annual swap payments and ignoring conventions/spreads for clarity:

Step 1. $Z(0, 1) = 1/(1 + 0.044 \cdot 1) = 0.9579$ (simple, 1y BBSW).
Step 2. The 2y par swap: $1 = 0.021 \cdot Z(0, 0.5) + 0.021 \cdot Z(0,1) + 0.021 \cdot Z(0, 1.5) + 1.021 \cdot Z(0, 2)$. If we approximate $Z(0, 0.5) \approx 0.978$ and $Z(0, 1.5) \approx 0.940$ (interpolated), we can solve $Z(0, 2)$ from $1 - 0.021(0.978 + 0.9579 + 0.940) = 1.021 \cdot Z(0, 2)$, giving $Z(0, 2) \approx 0.920$.
Step 3. Same procedure for the 3y swap, treating $Z(0, 2)$ as known and solving for $Z(0, 3)$.

In practice this is done iteratively in a spreadsheet or pricing system; conventions (which dates, which daycount, with or without basis spread) make it messier than the toy version. But the principle is exactly this: each new instrument adds one node, and each node solves for one unknown.

Module 3.1The Short-Rate World

Below 2 years, the market is dominated by short-rate instruments — bank bills, overnight index swaps, and forward rate agreements. They live in a parallel universe to bond pricing with different conventions, different counterparties, and different sensitivities. They are also where most monetary policy expectations get priced.

When we talk about "the curve," what we really mean below 2 years is a network of interrelated short-rate benchmarks. The cash rate (RBA target) anchors the very front; AONIA realises it daily; OIS extends the cash-rate expectations forward in time; BBSW adds bank credit on top; FRAs and futures lock in specific future windows. Understanding the differences between these instruments — and the spreads between them — is essential for any rates work in Australia.

6.1 BBSW — the bank bill swap rate

Definition · BBSW

The Bank Bill Swap Rate is a benchmark for unsecured bank borrowing at tenors of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 months. It is the rate at which prime banks issue 90-day bank bills (and their related tenors) into the wholesale market.

BBSW is computed daily by ASX from observed bid and offer rates in the bank bill market. The methodology is a "Volume-Weighted Average Price" of trades plus best bids/offers in an early-morning observation window. The result is published at 10:30am Sydney time.

BBSW is the closest Australian equivalent to USD LIBOR, but with structural differences. It's based on actually-transacted prices (or at least transactable bids/offers) rather than purely on dealer panel submissions — the methodology was reformed after the LIBOR manipulation scandal in 2014 to make it transaction-anchored. The 3-month BBSW is the reference for most AUD floating rate notes and is the floating leg of most AUD interest rate swaps. Its presence everywhere — in floating-rate corporate debt, securitisations, and swap legs — makes its level and movements central to the Australian short-rate market.

Example — pricing a 3-month bank bill

A 90-day bank bill has face value \$100. If the 3-month BBSW is 4.50%, the bill price is:

$$ P = \frac{100}{1 + 0.045 \times 90/365} = \frac{100}{1.01110} = \$98.902. $$

Simple interest, act/365 — the standard short-end convention.

6.2 OIS and AONIA

Definition · AONIA and OIS

AONIA (Australian Overnight Index Average) is the published reference rate for actual overnight unsecured interbank lending. It's the closest measurable proxy to "the RBA cash rate" — the realised market clearing of the policy target.

An Overnight Index Swap (OIS) exchanges a fixed rate for the compounded overnight AONIA realisations over the swap period. It is the cleanest available proxy for risk-free rates at term: no credit (overnight lending between top-tier banks is essentially default-free), no liquidity premium, only "what the market thinks AONIA will average over the period."

OIS rates are quoted by tenor (1m, 3m, 6m, 1y, 2y, ..., 30y). Each OIS rate is, in expectation, the average of AONIA realisations over its term. Because AONIA tracks the RBA target very closely, the OIS curve is the market's term structure of expected cash rates. This is the cleanest possible representation of monetary policy expectations.

OIS pricing — the compounding leg

For an OIS with fixed rate $K$ and floating leg paying compounded AONIA over $n$ days $t_1, \dots, t_n$ in the period (act/365):

$$ \text{floating} = \prod_{i=1}^n \left(1 + \frac{\text{AONIA}_i \cdot d_i}{365}\right) - 1, $$

where $d_i$ is the number of days the rate applies (1 for normal weekdays, 3 for the Friday rate covering the weekend). Cleaner approximation: floating $\approx (\bar r) \cdot T$ where $\bar r$ is the time-weighted average AONIA.

6.3 The BBSW-OIS spread

BBSW and OIS reference different things over the same tenor: BBSW reflects bank credit (unsecured 3-month bank bills); OIS reflects overnight risk-free rates. The spread between them is a barometer of stress in bank funding markets.

BBSW

Compensation for: holding a 3-month bank bill (so bank credit risk) and locking in cash for the full term (liquidity premium). Cannot be hedged perfectly by overnight funding because of mismatch in credit and term.

vs
OIS

Compensation for: expected cash rate path. No term credit risk (each overnight realisation is essentially risk-free), no liquidity premium (you don't lock up capital overnight beyond settlement). A pure "expectations" object.

The decomposition $$ \text{BBSW} \approx \text{OIS} + \text{credit premium} + \text{liquidity premium}. $$

In calm times the spread sits at 10–25bp. During stress (the GFC, March 2020) it blew out to 100bp+. The spread is the single best summary of "how worried is the market about bank funding."

Example — interpreting the spread

On 16 March 2020, 3-month BBSW was 0.65% and 3-month OIS was 0.21%. The 44bp spread reflected acute concerns that banks would have difficulty rolling over wholesale funding through the looming crisis. The RBA's response — a March 2020 cash rate cut to 0.25%, an unlimited Term Funding Facility for banks at 0.25%, and large-scale ACGB purchases — was largely aimed at compressing this spread back to normal. By July 2020 the BBSW-OIS spread was back below 15bp.

6.4 Forward rate agreements (FRAs)

Definition · FRA

A Forward Rate Agreement (FRA) is a single-period over-the-counter contract that fixes a future short-term rate. A 3×6 FRA, for instance, fixes the 3-month rate starting in 3 months — i.e., for the period from month 3 to month 6.

At the start of the rate period, the FRA settles in cash: the buyer of the FRA receives (or pays) the difference between the contracted rate $K$ and the realised reference rate $R$ over the period, discounted to the start of the period.

FRA settlement $$ \text{Payoff to FRA buyer} = N \cdot (R - K) \cdot \frac{\tau}{1 + R \tau}, $$

where $N$ is notional, $\tau$ is the daycount fraction of the rate period (e.g. 90/365), and $R$ is the realised reference rate (typically 3-month BBSW). The denominator $1 + R\tau$ discounts the rate-period payoff back to the start of the period (since that's the actual settlement date).

The FRA is the simplest forward-rate instrument. It locks in a rate today for a future period without any cash changing hands until the start of that period. This makes it the natural building block for curve work: a strip of FRAs with consecutive periods spans the same forward-rate space as a strip of futures contracts (Module 3.2).

The convexity adjustment lives here

Futures contracts and FRAs reference the same forward rate, but futures settle with daily margin variation and FRAs settle once at the start of the period. The daily-margin feature creates a slight asymmetry that makes futures rates systematically higher than forward rates. This is the convexity adjustment, covered in Module 3.2. It matters for long-dated curve construction — at 10-year tenor, the adjustment is several basis points.

Module 3.2Bond and Rate Futures

Futures sit in a strange position: they look like bonds, trade like equity index futures, settle in cash, and embed conventions you've never seen anywhere else. They are also the most liquid rates instruments in Australia by a wide margin. Master the conventions and you master the most active part of the AUD curve.

An ACGB cash bond trades a few hundred million dollars on a good day. The 10-year XM futures contract trades over ten billion dollars of equivalent notional in the same time. Almost all active rate-direction trading in Australia happens through futures, not bonds. Knowing what a futures contract is — and how its price relates to the cash bonds it tracks — is unavoidable.

7.1 The YM and XM bond futures

Definition · the ASX 24 bond futures suite

The ASX trades cash-settled bond futures referencing the Australian government curve:

  • YM — 3-year ACGB futures. Notional \$100,000 face. Quoted as 100 − yield. Tick size 0.005 (half a basis point); tick value A\$33.
  • XM — 10-year ACGB futures. Notional \$100,000 face. Same quoting convention; tick value A\$78 (larger because of higher duration).
  • VX — 20-year ACGB futures. Less liquid; same conventions.
  • IR — 90-day bank bill futures (the "BABs"). Quoted as 100 − rate; tick value A\$24.66 per basis point.

Contracts expire quarterly (March, June, September, December). Settlement is cash, based on the price of an underlying basket of deliverable bonds on the expiry date.

The 3-year (YM) and 10-year (XM) contracts are the two most important; together they account for the bulk of AUD rate-futures volume. They are cash-settled — there's no physical delivery of bonds at expiry. Instead, the final settlement price is computed from a basket of cash bonds whose maturities cluster around the contract's notional tenor.

7.2 The 100-minus-yield quoting convention

Bond futures in Australia are quoted as $\text{Price} = 100 - \text{Yield}$, where the yield is in percent. If the 10-year ACGB futures are trading at 95.80, the implied yield is $100 - 95.80 = 4.20\%$.

This convention is peculiar to Australia (and a couple of other markets). Most futures contracts on bonds elsewhere — the US Treasury futures, German Bund futures — quote in price units that resemble the underlying bond's price. The "100 minus yield" convention has two consequences worth understanding:

Direct yield reading

The price of the futures contract is the yield, with sign reversed. So a sell-off in futures (price down) is a rise in yield (yield up) — directly visible without any price-yield conversion. This is convenient for the rates trader who thinks in yields, not prices.

Why this matters for tick sizes

A 1bp move in yield is a 0.01 move in the contract price (since the contract is yield-scaled). The tick size is 0.005, so each tick is half a basis point. For the YM, a 1bp yield move is a \$66 P&L on a single contract; for the XM, it's \$156. These numbers are smaller than they'd be at the cash bond's true DV01 — because the futures price is yield-linear, not bond-price-linear. The "real" DV01 of the contract reflects the underlying bond's duration but is converted by the tick value.

Example — XM tick value calculation

Consider an XM contract on a 10-year ACGB with face \$100,000 and modified duration $\approx 8$. A 1bp yield change moves the underlying bond by $100,000 \times 8 \times 0.0001 = \$80$. The tick value on the ASX 24 specification is approximately \$78 (slight differences from the simple calc are conventional). One tick (half a basis point) is \$39 — the published value.

7.3 The deliverable basket and the ESP

Each bond futures contract doesn't reference a single cash bond — it references a basket of deliverable bonds whose maturities cluster around the notional tenor. For the YM (3-year), the basket might include all on-the-run ACGBs with maturities between 2.5 and 4.0 years; for the XM (10-year), bonds maturing between 8.5 and 11.5 years (the exact criteria are set by ASX each contract cycle).

The Exchange Settlement Price (ESP)

At expiry, each basket bond is assigned a yield based on a sample of dealer quotes over a defined observation window. These yields are averaged across the basket bonds (with appropriate conversion factors weighting them to the notional maturity). The averaged yield is then converted to a price via the standard 100−yield rule. That final price is the Exchange Settlement Price, and it determines the final cash settlement of every open futures contract.

The basket structure is a design feature: it prevents "squeeze" attacks (where a single trader corners the supply of one deliverable bond and forces the futures price away from fair value). With multiple deliverable bonds and a yield-average rule, no single bond's scarcity dominates the settlement.

Deep dive — conversion factors and the cheapest-to-deliver

For physically-delivered bond futures (like US Treasuries), each deliverable bond has a conversion factor — a number that adjusts the bond's price to make different coupons and maturities equivalent for delivery. The short of the futures chooses which bond to deliver; rationally, they pick the bond that is cheapest after conversion. This bond is the cheapest-to-deliver, and it dominates the futures price.

Australian YM/XM avoid this complication by cash-settling on a yield-averaged basket — there's no physical delivery, so no CTD optimisation. The trade-off: simpler settlement, but the futures track the basket average rather than any single bond. For a portfolio hedger this is usually fine; for an arbitrageur trying to lock in a specific bond's price-futures relationship, it adds basis risk.

7.4 The convexity adjustment

A futures contract and a forward rate agreement reference the same underlying rate, over the same future window. You might expect them to trade at exactly the same rate. They don't. The systematic difference between them is the convexity adjustment.

The mechanism: futures are marked to market daily. If you're long a futures contract and rates fall, the futures price rises, and your margin account gets credited the gain immediately. If rates rise, you pay the loss immediately. The forward contract has no such daily settlement — it pays once at maturity.

Now think about what daily margining does to the holder of a futures contract. When rates rally (gains on the long), you get cash early — which you can re-invest at the now-lower rate. When rates sell off (losses), you have to fund the loss — at the now-higher rate. This is an asymmetry against the long: gains arrive when reinvestment rates are low, losses must be funded when borrowing rates are high. The futures must therefore trade at a slightly higher rate than the forward to compensate the long for this asymmetry.

The Ho-Lee convexity adjustment

Under the Ho-Lee normal short-rate model, the futures rate exceeds the forward rate by approximately:

$$ r_{\text{forward}} = r_{\text{futures}} + \text{CA}, \qquad \text{CA} \approx -\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 T_1 T_2, $$

where $T_1$ is the time to the start of the rate period, $T_2$ is the time to the end, and $\sigma$ is the volatility of the short rate (in absolute, "normal" terms — basis points per √year). CA is negative because the forward is lower than the futures.

Example — convexity adjustment at various tenors

At a typical $\sigma \approx 100$bp normal volatility (1.0% per √year):

  • Front contract (3m to 6m forward): $\text{CA} \approx -\tfrac{1}{2}(0.01)^2 \cdot 0.25 \cdot 0.5 = -0.0000063 \approx -0.06\text{bp}$. Negligible.
  • 2-year forward 3-month rate: $\text{CA} \approx -\tfrac{1}{2}(0.01)^2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2.25 = -0.000225 \approx -2\text{bp}$. A few bp.
  • 5-year forward 3-month: $\text{CA} \approx -\tfrac{1}{2}(0.01)^2 \cdot 5 \cdot 5.25 = -0.000131 \approx -13\text{bp}$. About 10–15bp.
  • 10-year forward 3-month: $\text{CA} \approx -\tfrac{1}{2}(0.01)^2 \cdot 10 \cdot 10.25 = -0.000513 \approx -51\text{bp}$. About 40–60bp depending on vol.

The CA scales with $\sigma^2$: doubling rate volatility quadruples the adjustment. During high-vol regimes (2022), the CA at the long end of the futures strip can exceed 100bp, materially changing what curve-building from futures implies for forward rates.

Where this bites you in practice

If you're bootstrapping a curve from a futures strip and you forget the CA, your implied forward rates will be too high by the cumulative adjustment. At the 10-year point, this can mis-price a swap by tens of basis points — far more than any other convention error. Every modern pricing system applies the CA automatically; if you're doing the calc by hand, don't forget it.

Module 3.3Repo

Repo is the plumbing inside the plumbing. Every bond position needs financing; every futures arbitrage needs a way to lock in the bond at a known cost; every central bank operation uses repo as the mechanism. It's invisible most days and the centre of the universe when it breaks.

When a dealer holds a \$100 million ACGB position overnight, they don't fund it with their own capital. They fund it via repo — a short-term collateralised loan, secured by the bond itself. Repo is how the fixed income market generates liquidity, how short positions are financed, and how the RBA injects or drains cash from the banking system. This module is about the mechanics and the pricing.

8.1 Mechanics of a repo trade

Definition · repurchase agreement (repo)

A repo is a sale of a security with an agreement to buy it back at a fixed price on a fixed future date. Economically: a collateralised loan. The seller receives cash today, gives bonds today, pays a small rate of interest, and gets the bonds back at the end.

From the other side: a reverse repo is the same trade looked at from the cash-lender's perspective. You give cash, receive bonds as collateral, and at maturity you give the bonds back and receive cash plus interest.

Figure 8.1 — Repo lifecycle: cash flows one way at open, the other way at close; bonds move with them as collateral.

The repo rate is the interest rate paid on the cash leg. Australian repo is typically transacted as overnight ("O/N") or 1-week to 1-month term repo. Most repo trades are general collateral (GC) — the borrower can deliver any bond from a broadly defined eligibility list (typically all ACGBs and major semis). GC repo trades very close to the cash rate; the spread to AONIA is usually a couple of basis points and represents the small operational and collateral-management cost of running repo.

8.2 GC vs specials

Most repo is GC — but some bonds become specials, meaning they are in unusual demand and trade at a special (lower) repo rate. The mechanism: when many traders are short a particular bond, they all need to borrow it in repo to deliver into their short. The bond becomes scarce in the lending market; lenders can demand a lower repo rate (effectively earning extra by lending out the bond at a discount).

Specials and the specialness spread

A bond trades special when its repo rate is materially below GC. The difference is the specialness spread:

$$ s_{\text{special}} = r_{\text{GC}} - r_{\text{special}}. $$

Wide specialness spreads indicate that the bond is heavily shorted, or that it is scarce relative to demand. The on-the-run benchmark bond often trades a few bp special in normal conditions; in tight markets the spread can reach 50–100bp.

Why specials matter for arb traders

Suppose you spot a basis trade between the XM futures and the 10-year cheapest-to- deliver bond — the bond looks 5bp rich relative to where futures imply it should be. To arbitrage, you sell the bond and buy futures. Selling the bond means borrowing it in repo. If the bond is special at, say, 25bp below GC, your borrowing cost has just eaten your 5bp arb several times over. Specialness makes apparent arbitrage opportunities illusory — the bond was already priced rich to reflect the borrowing cost.

8.3 Implied repo rate and cash-and-carry

If you buy a bond today and sell a matching futures contract, you've locked in a return. The realised return is the bond's yield minus the cost of holding the bond (the repo rate) over the period to futures expiry. This is the implied repo rate.

Implied repo rate $$ \text{IRR} = \frac{(F + \text{accrued}_T) - (P_0 + \text{accrued}_0)}{P_0 + \text{accrued}_0} \cdot \frac{365}{\text{days}}, $$

where $F$ is the futures price (converted to a bond price equivalent), $P_0$ is the spot bond clean price today, and "days" is the time to futures expiry. The IRR is the annualised return on a fully-financed long-bond-short-futures position.

In equilibrium, the IRR should equal the actual repo rate available for the bond. If IRR > actual repo rate, you can borrow at repo and deploy at the bond's IRR for a riskless profit (this is the cash-and-carry arbitrage). Markets discipline IRR ≈ actual repo rate, after adjusting for specialness and conventions.

"Riskless" assumes the basket is unchanged

The cash-and-carry trade isn't quite riskless for ASX bond futures because the basket bonds shift as the contract approaches expiry, and a basket re-weighting can move the futures relative to your specific cash bond. This is called basket risk. In the US Treasury market, the equivalent is delivery option risk on physically-settled contracts. Either way, the "arb" is an arb against the average futures behaviour, not a locked-in static profit.

Module 3.4Interest Rate Swaps and EFP

The interest-rate swap is the most-traded financial instrument in the world. In Australia it's how every corporate borrower hedges fixed-rate exposure, how every super fund matches liabilities, and how every dealer expresses a curve view. The mechanics are simpler than they look — once you see them, every other derivative makes more sense.

A swap exchanges one cashflow stream for another. The "vanilla" AUD interest-rate swap (IRS) exchanges fixed coupons for floating coupons indexed to 3-month BBSW, over a matched-tenor period. The economic content: one party is converting a floating-rate exposure into fixed (or vice versa) without actually borrowing or lending. The swap is what makes that possible.

9.1 The vanilla AUD interest rate swap

Definition · vanilla AUD IRS

Two legs, both on the same notional $N$, over the same tenor $T$:

  • Fixed leg: pays a fixed rate $K$ semi-annually (act/365), so each payment is $N \cdot K \cdot \tau_i$ where $\tau_i \approx 0.5$.
  • Floating leg: pays 3-month BBSW quarterly, where BBSW is fixed at the start of each quarter and paid at the end. Each payment is $N \cdot \text{BBSW}_i \cdot \tau_i$ where $\tau_i \approx 0.25$.

The notional is never exchanged; only the cashflow differences. The two legs match in notional and tenor, so at any payment date you just net the two payments and one party pays the other.

By convention, the party that "pays fixed and receives float" is the payer of the swap; the counterparty is the receiver. A payer is short duration (paying fixed, exposed to floating rates rising); a receiver is long duration (paying float, earning fixed). This is the most common framing on a trading desk: "I'm a 10y swap receiver of \$100m" means long duration.

Swap value (mid-life)

At any point during the swap's life, its value to the receiver is the PV of the fixed leg minus the PV of the floating leg:

$$ V_{\text{receiver}} = K \sum_{i} \tau_i Z(0, t_i) \cdot N - \sum_{j} F_j \tau_j Z(0, s_j) \cdot N, $$

where $K$ is the fixed rate, $F_j$ is the forward BBSW projected for floating period $j$, and the $Z$ values are OIS discount factors. The floating-leg sum can be rewritten using forward rates from the projection curve.

9.2 The par swap rate

The par swap rate is the value of $K$ that makes a new swap initiate at zero NPV. Two parties enter a fresh swap and neither pays nor receives any premium up front. The par rate is found by setting the two legs equal:

Par swap rate formula $$ K_{\text{par}} = \frac{\sum_j F_j \tau_j Z(0, s_j)}{\sum_i \tau_i Z(0, t_i)} = \frac{Z(0, t_0) - Z(0, T)}{\sum_i \tau_i Z(0, t_i)}, $$

where the second equality uses the fact that the floating leg's PV equals $N \cdot (Z(0, t_0) - Z(0, T))$ in continuous discounting (which simplifies to "1 minus the terminal discount factor" if the swap starts now). The denominator is the annuity factor: the PV of receiving \$1 per year, paid in installments, over the swap's life.

Three takeaways from this formula. First, the par swap rate is a weighted average of forward BBSW rates — exactly the kind of average that the OIS curve also represents (with some BBSW spread on top). Second, the par swap rate is a benchmark in itself: it's what gets quoted on screens, and the spread between the par swap rate and the same- tenor government bond yield is the swap spread. Third, after inception the swap's value moves around as rates change — and that movement is what makes a swap a duration product.

9.3 Asset swap spread

A common trade: buy a bond and simultaneously enter a swap that converts the bond's fixed coupon into a floating stream of BBSW + spread. The result is a synthetic floating-rate note. The spread paid on top of BBSW is the asset swap spread, and it is a clean measure of the credit and liquidity premium embedded in the bond's yield relative to BBSW.

Asset swap spread (par-par convention)

For a bond with coupon $C$ and price $P$, swapped to floating, the par-par asset swap spread $s_{\text{asw}}$ satisfies:

$$ \text{coupons} - 100 + \text{accrued} = \text{floating leg} + s_{\text{asw}} \cdot \text{annuity}. $$

Solving for $s_{\text{asw}}$ gives the spread that the asset swap pays over BBSW on the floating side, in exchange for delivering the bond's economics.

Reading a positive vs negative ASW

A 10-year ACGB might trade at an asset swap spread of -10bp — i.e. the floating-rate equivalent yields less than 3m BBSW. This is normal for AAA-rated government bonds, which are perceived as safer than (and demand a lower yield than) bank-credit BBSW.

A 10-year corporate bond (say a BBB-rated Australian utility) might trade at an asset swap spread of +120bp. The corporate's credit risk requires a 120bp premium over BBSW. This is a much cleaner measure of corporate credit risk than just looking at the bond's yield, because BBSW already embeds the bank-credit context.

9.4 EFP — Exchange for Physical

Definition · EFP

An Exchange for Physical (EFP) is a packaged trade: simultaneous purchase of a cash bond and sale of bond futures (or vice versa) at an agreed spread. It's quoted in yield-spread units: "10-year EFP is 25bp" means the cash bond yield is 25bp above the futures-implied yield.

EFP exists because cash bonds and bond futures track each other closely but not perfectly. Dealers maintain inventory of cash bonds and need to hedge with futures; buy-side accounts want to express views on the cash-futures spread. EFP gives them a single quote for the package. EFP is also the cleanest way to roll a hedge from a near-expiry futures contract to the next quarter without flow-driven slippage.

Tenor mismatch and the EFP curve

The catch: AUD bond futures have fixed notional tenors (3y for YM, 10y for XM). A particular cash bond might be 3.5 years to maturity — falling between the two contracts. In that case the EFP trade has a tenor mismatch: the cash bond's duration and the futures' duration aren't equal, so a single-contract hedge will have residual duration risk.

The fix: use a weighted combination of YM and XM contracts to match the cash bond's duration. If the cash bond has duration 3.5 years and YM duration is 2.8, XM duration is 8.5, then the weights $w_{YM}$ and $w_{XM}$ on the two contracts satisfy $w_{YM} \cdot 2.8 + w_{XM} \cdot 8.5 = 3.5$, and conventionally $w_{YM} + w_{XM} = 1$. Solving gives $w_{XM} = (3.5 - 2.8)/(8.5 - 2.8) = 0.123$ and $w_{YM} = 0.877$ — almost all YM, with a small XM overlay.

Curve risk in EFP

A duration-weighted EFP hedge is parallel-shift-neutral but not curve-shape-neutral. If the curve twists (2-year and 10-year move in opposite directions), the cash bond and the futures combination will respond differently and you'll have a P&L. Sophisticated EFP shops use key-rate hedging to reduce this risk.

Module 4.1 · FX forwards & the cross-currency basis

Module 4.1FX and Cross-Currency

Australia is a small open economy with a freely floating currency, so almost every fixed-income position touches FX at some point. Covered interest parity should make the link trivial; in practice, a persistent cross-currency basis turns it into a tradable market in its own right.

When an offshore investor buys an AUD bond, they have to manage two risks: the bond's rate risk (their old story), and the AUD/USD exchange rate risk (a new story). The forward FX market exists to manage the second. The pricing of that forward is, in theory, fully determined by interest rates — but post-2008 there's a wedge between theory and reality. That wedge is the cross-currency basis, and trading it has become a multi-trillion-dollar business.

10.1 Covered interest parity

The starting point. Suppose today's spot AUD/USD is $S$ (USD per AUD). You have \$100 USD and you want to know: should you invest in USD at rate $r_d$ (domestic, USD) for a year, or convert to AUD, invest at rate $r_f$ (foreign, AUD), then convert back at the forward rate $F$?

In a no-arbitrage world the two strategies must yield the same dollar amount. From this:

Covered Interest Parity (CIP) $$ F = S \cdot \frac{1 + r_d \cdot t}{1 + r_f \cdot t}, $$

where $r_d$ and $r_f$ are the domestic and foreign rates with daycount $t$. For USD, use act/360; for AUD, use act/365. CIP says the forward FX rate is fully determined by the spot rate and the rate differential — there's no extra economic content.

Pre-2008 this held to within a few basis points across all major pairs. CIP was so robust that traders used it as a definitional relationship — the forward rate was defined as the right-hand-side. Any deviation was an arbitrage to be exploited and would close within seconds.

Example — pre-2008 CIP applied

Spot AUD/USD = 0.6500. 1-year USD rate = 5.00% (act/360). 1-year AUD rate = 4.00% (act/365). To compute the 1-year forward:

$$ F = 0.6500 \cdot \frac{1 + 0.05 \cdot 365/360}{1 + 0.04 \cdot 1} = 0.6500 \cdot \frac{1.05069}{1.04} = 0.6566. $$

The AUD trades at a forward premium (higher in 1y than spot) because the USD rate is higher than the AUD rate. The forward premium compensates the AUD-holder for earning the lower rate.

10.2 The cross-currency basis

Post-2008, CIP started to fail in a systematic and persistent way. By 2015, the AUD/USD basis at 5 years had widened to over 50bp — i.e., the right-hand-side of CIP was 50bp off the left-hand-side, and this gap stayed for years.

The cross-currency basis $$ \text{basis}_{\text{AUDUSD}}(t) = r_f^{\text{implied}}(t) - r_f^{\text{actual}}(t), $$

where $r_f^{\text{implied}}$ is the AUD rate that CIP would require to match observed forward FX, and $r_f^{\text{actual}}$ is the observed AUD rate. The basis is the wedge. A negative basis means the implied AUD rate is below the actual AUD rate — the AUD is "cheap" in the FX forward market relative to what its rates suggest.

Where does the basis come from? Two main sources, both connected to post-2008 changes in bank balance sheets. First, regulatory capital requirements (Basel III's leverage ratio and supplementary capital charges) made it expensive for banks to expand their balance sheets — and the CIP arb requires balance sheet (you borrow in one currency, lend in another, with corresponding gross assets and liabilities). Second, persistent funding demand: hedging the FX risk of offshore-investor flows into AUD bonds creates structural demand to lock in forward USD/sell forward AUD, which presses down on the implied USD rate.

10.3 Cross-currency swaps (CCS)

Definition · cross-currency basis swap

A cross-currency basis swap exchanges floating-rate cashflows in two currencies. The standard AUD/USD CCS pays 3m BBSW + basis spread in AUD, against 3m SOFR (or USD LIBOR historically) in USD, on respective notionals exchanged at start and reversed at end. The "basis spread" is the basis we just discussed — usually quoted in basis points on the non-USD leg.

CCS is the market's main mechanism for trading the basis. If you're an offshore investor buying AUD bonds and hedging FX, you implicitly pay the basis. If you're an Australian bank issuing USD debt and swapping back to AUD, you implicitly earn the basis. The bilateral nature of these flows determines the equilibrium basis level.

Example — offshore hedger paying the basis

A US pension fund buys \$100m of 10-year ACGB and wants to hedge the AUD exposure with a 10-year CCS. They effectively go: receive AUD fixed (from the bond), pay AUD BBSW + basis (CCS leg), receive USD SOFR (CCS leg), pay USD fixed (their own funding cost). If the 10y basis is -25bp, they pay an extra 25bp per year on the AUD leg of the CCS compared to CIP-implied. Annualised cost of hedging: roughly the basis times the notional. This is real money — 25bp on \$100m is \$250,000 per year, or \$2.5m over the 10-year holding period.

Module 5.1 · Credit spreads & CDS

Module 5.1Credit and CDS

Once you leave the safe haven of government bonds, every cashflow comes with the risk that the issuer can't pay it. Credit spreads measure that risk; credit default swaps let you trade it cleanly. The CDS-bond basis sits between them and is the desk's favourite cross-asset arbitrage.

A government bond is (for practical purposes) default-free. A corporate bond is not. The yield differential — the credit spread — is the market's price of taking that risk. This module is about how to think about the spread, how to trade it via CDS, and the interesting relationship between the two.

11.1 The credit spread, decomposed

Definition · credit spread $$ s = y_{\text{corp}} - y_{\text{govt}}, $$

the yield premium of a corporate bond over a matched-maturity government bond. Sometimes quoted as a "Z-spread" — the parallel shift to the swap curve that makes the corporate's discounted cashflows match its price.

The credit spread isn't pure default-loss compensation. It's a mixture of several risks that the market lumps together because they're hard to disentangle ex ante:

ComponentWhat it compensates for
Expected default lossThe probability of default times the loss given default. Mathematically: $\lambda \cdot (1 - R)$ where $\lambda$ is the hazard rate and $R$ the recovery rate.
Credit risk premiumCompensation for bearing unexpected credit losses. Investors require more than the expected loss; how much more depends on risk appetite.
Liquidity premiumCompensation for the bid-ask costs and potential difficulty exiting the position. Wider for less-traded corporates.
Tax and structural factorsTreatment differences, callable features, embedded options.
Reading a 150bp spread

A BBB-rated 10-year corporate trades at +150bp over Treasury. Historical default rates for BBB at 10y are roughly 5% cumulative, with average recovery ~40%, so the expected-loss component is $\lambda(1-R) \approx 0.05/10 \cdot 0.60 = 30\text{bp}$. So 30bp is "fair compensation" for expected default; the other 120bp is the risk premium plus liquidity premium plus structural factors. Pure expected-loss models chronically underprice credit risk because they ignore the risk-premium component.

11.2 Credit Default Swaps (CDS)

Definition · single-name CDS

A Credit Default Swap is a contract between two parties: the protection buyer pays a quarterly fixed premium (the "spread") to the protection seller, and the protection seller agrees to pay out a contingent amount if a specified credit event occurs on a specified reference entity.

If no credit event occurs, the buyer pays premium for the contract's life; nothing else happens. If a credit event occurs (bankruptcy, failure to pay, restructuring), the seller pays the buyer $(1 - R) \cdot N$, where $R$ is the recovery rate determined by auction and $N$ is the notional. The premium leg stops.

CDS is, in effect, insurance on a bond. The protection buyer is effectively short the reference entity's credit risk; the protection seller is long. The spread paid is the cost of the insurance — and it should approximately equal the credit spread of the reference entity's bonds, if both markets are efficient.

CDS-implied default probability

From the par CDS spread, you can back out the implied default probability. To first order:

$$ s_{\text{CDS}} \approx \lambda (1 - R), $$

where $\lambda$ is the hazard rate (annual default probability under risk-neutral measure). So if the 5y CDS spread is 300bp and recovery is assumed at 40%:

$$ \lambda \approx 0.03/0.6 = 0.05 = 5\% \text{ per year}. $$

This is the risk-neutral default probability — i.e., the rate you'd need to assume to make CDS pricing make sense. It's higher than the historical default rate, by the credit-risk-premium factor.

11.3 The CDS-bond basis

If a corporate's CDS spread is 200bp and its bonds trade at +180bp asset-swap spread, the difference (+20bp) is the CDS-bond basis. The basis is positive here: CDS protection is more expensive than the bond's apparent credit premium.

CDS-bond basis $$ \text{basis} = s_{\text{CDS}} - s_{\text{bond}}. $$

Positive basis: CDS richer than bond. Negative basis: bond richer than CDS. In theory, no-arb conditions would set the basis to zero — but in practice, the basis is typically 0 to ±50bp and trades as its own variable.

The basis trade: if the basis is significantly positive, you can buy the bond and buy CDS protection on it. The bond's coupon pays you the cash spread; the CDS protection costs you the CDS spread; the net cashflow is the bond spread minus CDS spread, which is negative — but you've hedged the credit risk. In return you keep the rate exposure (which can be hedged separately with swaps) and the funding spread of the bond, while bearing zero credit P&L. If the basis converges, you book the spread.

Why the basis isn't zero

Several frictions keep the basis from being arbitraged away. The bond is fundable; the CDS isn't (no balance sheet impact for the protection seller in the same way). The bond has counterparty risk (the issuer); the CDS has counterparty risk on a different entity (the protection seller). Recovery rates differ in practice (bond holders have legal claims; CDS auctions can produce different recoveries). Plus bonds and CDS have different liquidity, different convexity, and different documentation. The basis reflects all these idiosyncrasies — and trades within them.

End-of-module checks
  1. A 5-year A-rated corporate trades at +80bp over Treasury. Historical 5y default rate for A-rated is ~1%, recovery ~40%. What's the implied expected-loss component, and what does the rest of the spread compensate for?
    Reveal

    Expected loss: $0.01 \cdot 0.6 / 5 = 12\text{bp}$. So 12bp is the fair actuarial price of credit risk. The other 68bp is risk premium (investors demand more than the expected loss to bear unknown future credit risk) plus liquidity premium (corporate bonds are less liquid than Treasuries).

  2. A 5y CDS quotes at 200bp. Recovery is 40%. What's the implied risk-neutral default probability per year?
    Reveal

    $\lambda \approx s/(1-R) = 0.02/0.6 = 3.33\%$ per year, or about 15.4% cumulative over 5 years (using $1 - e^{-\lambda \cdot 5}$). Risk-neutral, so higher than the actuarial probability.

  3. A corporate's bonds trade at ASW +100bp, its CDS is at 130bp. What is the basis and what trade does this suggest?
    Reveal

    Basis = +30bp (CDS richer than bond). If you believe the basis will compress, sell CDS protection (collect 130bp) and short the bond (pay back 100bp ASW). Net carry: +30bp. The credit risk is hedged because the CDS sale offsets the bond short (both expose you to the issuer's default). You're capturing the basis.

  4. Why is the risk-neutral default probability higher than the historical default rate?
    Reveal

    Because the market demands a risk premium on top of expected loss. The risk-neutral measure prices contingent claims as if investors were risk-neutral — which they aren't. The wedge between risk-neutral and physical default probabilities is the market price of credit risk premium. Empirically this wedge is large: risk-neutral default rates can be 2–5× historical.

Module 5.1 (and Session 5) takeaways
  • Credit spread = corporate yield − govt yield. Mixes expected loss, risk premium, and liquidity premium.
  • Expected loss component ≈ default probability × (1 − recovery). Usually small relative to total spread.
  • CDS is insurance on default. Buyer pays premium; seller pays $(1-R) \cdot N$ on credit event.
  • CDS-implied default probability: $\lambda \approx s_{CDS}/(1-R)$. Risk-neutral, not historical.
  • CDS-bond basis = CDS spread − bond credit spread. Typically 0 to ±50bp.
  • Frictions (funding, counterparty, documentation, liquidity) keep the basis from being arb'd to zero.
  • Basis trade: long the cheap leg, short the rich leg, capture the convergence (if it comes).

Case 1 — Hedging an ACGB position with bond futures

Setup. A super fund is long \$200m face value of the 10-year ACGB (the Nov-2034). The bond has clean price 98.85, dirty price 99.42, modified duration 8.4, and DV01 per \$100 face of \$0.0831. The portfolio manager wants to neutralise rate risk for a month while they reassess. The XM 10-year futures has DV01 ≈ \$78 per contract.

Walk through

Step 1 — compute position DV01. $(200{,}000{,}000 / 100) \cdot 0.0831 = \$166{,}200$ per basis point.

Step 2 — divide by hedge DV01. $166{,}200 / 78 = 2{,}131$ contracts.

Step 3 — direction. Long the cash bond, so sell futures to hedge. Sell 2,131 XM contracts.

Step 4 — verify. 1bp parallel down move: bond gains $166{,}200$; futures short loses $2{,}131 \times 78 = \$166{,}218$. Net P&L = \$−18. Effectively flat. ✓

Residual. Twist risk remains — if the 2y rallies and 10y sells off, the cash bond moves differently from the XM combination of bonds in the basket. Key-rate hedging would split the trade across YM (some) and XM (most) to reduce this.

Case 2 — EFP with tenor mismatch

Setup. A dealer wants to do an EFP for a 4.0-year cash bond. The available futures are YM (3-year, duration 2.8) and XM (10-year, duration 8.5). The cash bond has duration 3.6. How should the futures hedge be split?

Walk through

Step 1 — weighting equation. $w_{YM} \cdot 2.8 + w_{XM} \cdot 8.5 = 3.6$, with $w_{YM} + w_{XM} = 1$.

Step 2 — solve. Substituting $w_{YM} = 1 - w_{XM}$:

$(1 - w_{XM}) \cdot 2.8 + w_{XM} \cdot 8.5 = 3.6$
$2.8 + w_{XM} \cdot (8.5 - 2.8) = 3.6$
$w_{XM} = 0.8/5.7 = 0.140$, $w_{YM} = 0.860$.

Step 3 — apply. 86% YM, 14% XM. For a \$100m bond position, roughly 860 YM contracts and 140 XM contracts (adjusting for individual contract DV01s in detail).

Residual. Duration-matched. Still has curve-shape risk: a 4y vs 3y vs 10y curve twist will not cancel out. For most EFP traders this is acceptable; for a portfolio carrying many such positions, key-rate hedging would be needed.

Case 3 — Implying RBA rate-cut probability from futures

Setup. The current cash rate is 4.10%. The 30-day cash rate futures for next month is pricing 100 − 4.05 = 95.95. There are 14 days until the next RBA meeting; the meeting will determine the cash rate for the remaining 16 days of the month (assume a 30-day month for simplicity). What's the market-implied probability of a 25bp cut?

Walk through

Step 1 — what the futures implies. Futures price of 95.95 corresponds to an average cash rate of 4.05% for the month.

Step 2 — average rate formula. Pre-meeting: rate = 4.10% for $d = 14$ days. Post-meeting: rate = $R_2$ for $N - d = 16$ days. Average: $$F = \frac{R_2 (N - d) + R_1 d}{N} \implies 4.05 = \frac{R_2 \cdot 16 + 4.10 \cdot 14}{30}.$$

Step 3 — solve for $R_2$. $R_2 = (4.05 \cdot 30 - 4.10 \cdot 14)/16 = (121.5 - 57.4)/16 = 4.006\%$. The market is implying the post-meeting cash rate at 4.006%.

Step 4 — implied probability. Two outcomes: hold at 4.10% or cut to 3.85%. Expected value = $p \cdot 3.85 + (1-p) \cdot 4.10 = 4.006$. Solve $p = (4.10 - 4.006)/(4.10 - 3.85) = 0.094/0.25 = 0.376$, so about 38% probability of a 25bp cut priced in.

Caveat. Assumes no probability of larger or smaller moves. In practice 50bp cuts are possible too — the implied 4.006 is the expectation, not specifically a probability for a single binary outcome.

Case 4 — Cash-and-carry on an ACGB-XM package

Setup. The 10-year ACGB has clean price 98.50, accrued 0.30 (settle), so dirty 98.80. The XM 10y futures (expiring in 60 days) trades at 95.70. Implied bond price at futures = $100 - 4.30 \cdot \text{duration}$ adjustment... actually we look it up: the conversion implies a forward bond price of 98.30 at expiry. Bond coupon income over 60 days: 0.42 per 100 face. Repo on the bond runs at 4.10%. Is there an arb?

Walk through

Step 1 — long-bond cashflows. Pay \$98.80 today. Sell at \$98.30 + 0.42 carry = \$98.72 in 60 days. Capital change: −\$0.08 per 100 face.

Step 2 — financing cost. Borrow \$98.80 at 4.10% for 60 days act/365: cost = $98.80 \cdot 0.041 \cdot 60/365 = \$0.666$ per 100 face.

Step 3 — net P&L. $-0.08 - 0.666 = -\$0.746$ per 100 face. Loss. The implied repo rate is much lower than actual: implied = $(\text{change}/P_0) \cdot 365/60 = (-0.08/98.80) \cdot 365/60 = -0.49\%$. The bond's IRR is far below GC.

Step 4 — interpretation. Either (i) the bond trades special (its actual repo is well below GC, justifying the apparent loss); (ii) basket dynamics — the XM doesn't exactly reflect this specific bond. The "arb" is illusory; the gap to GC reflects specialness or basket risk.

Case 5 — SOFR FRA convexity adjustment

Setup. The 2y2y SOFR futures rate (futures-implied forward 2-year rate starting in 2 years) is 4.50%. Volatility $\sigma \approx 100$bp normal. What's the convexity-adjusted forward rate to use for pricing a FRA at the same point?

Walk through

Step 1 — Ho-Lee formula. $\text{CA} \approx -\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 T_1 T_2$ where $T_1 = 2$, $T_2 = 4$.

Step 2 — plug in. $\text{CA} \approx -\tfrac{1}{2}(0.01)^2 \cdot 2 \cdot 4 = -0.000400 = -4\text{bp}$.

Step 3 — apply. Forward rate = futures rate + CA = 4.50% − 0.04% = 4.46%. Use 4.46% to price the FRA.

Sensitivity. If vol doubles to 200bp, CA becomes -16bp (quadruples with σ²). At long tenors with high vol, this is a material adjustment, not a rounding error.

Case 6 — Pricing a 5-year asset swap (SWPM-style)

Setup. A 5-year corporate bond, 4.50% coupon, semi-annual, trading at 101.50 clean (accrued 0.50, dirty 102.00). The 5-year par swap rate is 4.10%. What asset swap spread do you receive when you swap this bond to floating?

Walk through

Step 1 — what the package does. Buy the bond. Enter a swap where you pay the bond's coupon (4.50%) and receive BBSW + spread. The net cash flow is the floating (BBSW + spread) less the bond price premium paid.

Step 2 — par-par convention. Approximate spread (par-par) ≈ (bond coupon − par swap rate) − (price premium amortised over annuity factor). Bond coupon − swap rate = 4.50 − 4.10 = +40bp. Price premium of 1.50 (clean) amortised over the 5y annuity factor (~4.4 years) = 1.50/4.4 ≈ 34bp.

Step 3 — net spread. 40 − 34 = +6bp. So the asset swap spread on this bond is approximately 6bp over BBSW.

Interpretation. Very tight — typical for a low-credit-risk corporate. A higher-credit-risk name with the same coupon and price would have a wider spread because the bond yield itself would be higher.

One-page cheat sheet

Compounding conversions
  • Simple to continuous: $r_c = \ln(1 + r_s t)/t$.
  • Semi-annual to continuous: $r_c = 2\ln(1 + r_{sa}/2)$.
  • Discount factor: $Z(0,T) = e^{-rT}$ (continuous) or $(1+y/2)^{-2T}$ (semi).
  • Forward rate: $f(T_1, T_2) = (r_2 T_2 - r_1 T_1)/(T_2 - T_1)$.
Bond pricing (AOFM)
  • $P_{\text{dirty}} = v^f[g + g a_{\overline{n-1}|} + 100 v^{n-1}]$ where $v = (1+y/2)^{-1}$.
  • $g$ = coupon/2, $f$ = fraction of period to next coupon, $n$ = remaining periods.
  • Accrued = $g \cdot \tau$, where $\tau$ = days since last coupon / days in period.
  • Clean = Dirty − Accrued. Dealers quote clean; cash settles on dirty.
Risk measures
  • Modified duration: $D^* = D_{\text{Mac}}/(1 + y/n)$.
  • DV01 = $P \cdot D^* \cdot 0.0001$ (dollars per bp per face).
  • $\Delta P/P \approx -D^* \Delta y + \tfrac{1}{2} C (\Delta y)^2$.
  • Hedge ratio: $N = \text{DV01}_{\text{pos}}/\text{DV01}_{\text{hedge}}$.
Australian futures conventions
  • YM (3y), XM (10y): price = 100 − yield in %. Tick = 0.5bp.
  • YM tick value: $A33; XM tick value: $A39 (per half-bp).
  • Cash-settled via basket; no physical delivery, no CTD.
  • Ho-Lee CA: $\text{CA} \approx -\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 T_1 T_2$. Negligible front, ~50bp at 10y.
Short-rate landscape
  • Cash rate target → AONIA realised → OIS curve (expectations of cash).
  • BBSW = bank credit + liquidity. BBSW − OIS ≈ 15–25bp normally.
  • FRA payoff: $N(R-K)\tau/(1+R\tau)$ at start of rate period.
  • OIS is the discount curve; BBSW is the projection curve.
Swaps and asset swaps
  • Vanilla AUD IRS: 6m fixed vs 3m BBSW float. Payer is short duration.
  • Par rate: $K_{\text{par}} = (Z_0 - Z_T)/\sum \tau_i Z_i$.
  • Swap spread: $K - y_{\text{govt}}$. ≈ 15–30bp normal in AUD.
  • Asset swap spread: bond converted to floating yields BBSW + spread.
FX and cross-currency
  • CIP: $F = S(1 + r_d t)/(1 + r_f t)$. USD uses act/360, AUD act/365.
  • Basis: persistent post-2008. Negative for AUD/USD ⇒ AUD cheap forward.
  • Drivers: bank balance sheet costs + one-way hedging flows.
  • Offshore AUD bond hedgers pay the basis; AU banks issuing USD earn it.
Credit and CDS
  • Spread = expected loss + risk premium + liquidity premium.
  • Expected loss ≈ $\lambda (1-R)$. Usually small fraction of total spread.
  • CDS: $s_{\text{CDS}} \approx \lambda(1-R)$ implies risk-neutral default rate.
  • CDS-bond basis: CDS − bond spread. Frictions keep it from being arbed to zero.
